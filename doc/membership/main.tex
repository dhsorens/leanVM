\title{Zero-knowledge Merkle membership from two evaluation claims}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\noindent\textbf{In one paragraph.} Suppose the EVM had a precompile that checks one multilinear evaluation claim (commitment, point, value) on a WHIR commitment~\cite{ACFY25}, at a flat price, the openings travelling beside transactions and being aggregated before the block. Then a proof should be a short reduction of its statement to a few such claims. This note does that for one statement, a BLAKE2s preimage plus a Merkle path of depth 32, in zero knowledge: \textbf{98 field elements and a root (2{,}384 bytes), about 2{,}000 field multiplications to verify, two claims}. The verifier never reads the circuit's matrices. The code is \code{flock::membership}, and the verifier also exists as an EVM contract, measured in Section~\ref{sec:cost}.

\section{Statement}

Public: $y$ and $\rootnode$, 32 bytes each. Secret: $x$ (32 bytes), siblings $s_i$ and direction bits $d_i$, with $\hash$ = BLAKE2s:
\[
  \hash(x \concat \mathtt{0x11}^{32}) = y, \qquad
  h_0 = \hash(x \concat \mathtt{0x22}^{32}), \qquad
  h_i = \hash\bigl(\text{$h_{i-1}$ and $s_i$, ordered by $d_i$}\bigr), \qquad
  h_{32} = \rootnode .
\]
Every input is one 64-byte block, so this is 34 compressions.

\section{Circuit}

Fields: $\K = \mathrm{GF}(2^{64})$ for the witness, $\E = \mathrm{GF}(2^{192})$ for challenges and proof scalars (24 bytes).

It is an R1CS $(Az) \circ (Bz) = Cz$ over $\K$ with $2^{22}$ wires, row $\ell$ defining wire $\ell$. \textbf{A wire of the computation is one bit}, held as a whole element of $\K$: free inputs carry $z = z \cdot z$, and the rest is XORs and ANDs (a 32-bit addition is 31 carry rows). The only other wires are free field elements added for zero knowledge (below). Wasteful for the prover, but nothing needs unpacking: no ring switching, and public values are pinned by linear conditions.

The wires are 64 compressions (34 real) $\times$ 32 blocks $\times$ 2048 wires, so a wire index is a pair (position $\ell$ inside the block, block $j$). A block has one of three types $T(j)$: $\mathsf S$ (builds the message), $\mathsf H$ (half a BLAKE2s round, twenty per compression), $\mathsf F$ (final XOR); the unused blocks are zero.

$A$, $B$, $C$ are the full $2^{22} \times 2^{22}$ matrices. They are never written out, because they are made of two small things:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Local matrices} $A_T, B_T \in \{0,1\}^{2048 \times 2048}$, one pair per type $T$: what a row reads inside its own block. Every block of type $T$ repeats the same pair.
  \item \textbf{Wiring} $W$, a 0/1 matrix with a single 1 per copy row: a block reading a wire of another block (the previous state, a message word through the schedule, the previous hash).
\end{itemize}
In block form,
\[
  A = \mathrm{diag}_j\bigl(A_{T(j)}\bigr) + W, \qquad B = \mathrm{diag}_j\bigl(B_{T(j)}\bigr) .
\]
The three pairs $(A_T, B_T)$ are the only circuit data worth committing: one table $\idx$ of 25 variables holds $A_T$ and $A_T + B_T$ for each $T$, so that its last coordinate at $\alpha$ gives $A_T + \alpha B_T$. $W$ needs no commitment: it is a product of small maps on the index coordinates, so the verifier evaluates $\mle W$ in closed form.

$C$ is the identity, except on a few mask wires (below) where it is zero.

\section{Protocol}

Let $n = 22$. The prover commits $P = (z, m_0, m_1, m_2)$: the witness and three random tables over $\K$. The transcript, a Keccak-256 chain, is seeded with the commitment to $\idx$, $y$ and $\rootnode$. Write $a = Az$, $b = Bz$, $c = Cz$. The skeleton is Flock's zerocheck then lincheck~\cite{Flock26}.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.5em]
  \item \textbf{Zerocheck.} With a mask $g(x) = g_\varnothing + \sum_i (g_i x_i + g'_i x_i^2)$ whose coefficients in $\E$ are wires of $z$ (three each), the verifier sends $r$, the prover sends $S = \sum_x \eq(r,x) g(x)$, the verifier sends $\lambda$, and they sumcheck
  \[
    \sum_{x} \eq(r, x) \bigl[\, \mle a(x)\, \mle b(x) + \mle c(x) + \lambda\, g(x) \,\bigr] = \lambda S ,
  \]
  two scalars a round, down to a point $\rho$. The prover sends $\hat a = \mle a(\rho)$, $\hat b = \mle b(\rho)$, $\gamma = g(\rho)$; the verifier \emph{derives} $\hat c$ from the final claim. Left over: four claims, all linear in $z$.

  \item \textbf{Lincheck.} A random combination (challenges $\alpha$ and $\beta$) turns those four claims, plus ``the constant wire of every used block is 1'', ``these wires hold $\mathtt{0x11}^{32}$, $\mathtt{0x22}^{32}$, $y$, $\rootnode$'' and ``the first two direction bits are 0'', into one:
  \[
    \sum_{y} \comb(y)\, z(y) = v, \qquad \comb(y) = \sum_x \eq(\rho, x) (A + \alpha B + \alpha^2 C)(x, y) + \dots,
  \]
  the dots being the weights of $g(\rho)$ and of the fixed wires.
  The prover sends $S_k = \sum_y \comb(y) m_k(y)$, the verifier sends $\mu$, and the sumcheck runs on $t = z + \mu m_0 + \mu^2 m_1 + \mu^3 m_2$ against $v + \mu S_0 + \mu^2 S_1 + \mu^3 S_2$, two scalars a round, down to a point $q$ and a value $\mle\comb(q) \cdot \mle t(q)$.

  \item \textbf{Two claims.} The prover sends three scalars $V_T = \mle\idx(\rho_\loc, q_\loc, T, \alpha)$, one per block type, where $\rho_\loc$ and $q_\loc$ are the coordinates inside a block. That is all the verifier needs of the circuit: by the block form above,
  \[
    \mle{(A + \alpha B)}(\rho, q) = \sum_{\text{used block } j} \eq(\rho_{\mathrm{blk}}, j)\, \eq(q_{\mathrm{blk}}, j)\, V_{T(j)} \;+\; \mle W(\rho, q),
  \]
  with $\rho_{\mathrm{blk}}$, $q_{\mathrm{blk}}$ the remaining coordinates. So it computes $\mle\comb(q)$ itself, hence $\mle t(q)$. It outputs:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item on the prover's commitment, $\mle t(q)$ as a single evaluation of $P$: the two extra coordinates set to $\mu/(1+\mu)$ and $\mu^2/(1+\mu^2)$ weight the four tables by $1, \mu, \mu^2, \mu^3$, up to a known factor;
    \item on the circuit's commitment, $\mle\idx(\rho_\loc, q_\loc, \tau, \alpha) = \sum_T \eq(\tau, T) V_T$ at a fresh random $\tau \in \E^2$, which checks the three $V_T$ at once.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The proof is $(1 + 2n + 3) + (3 + 2n + 3) = 98$ scalars and one root. The masks cost only a negligible soundness error: each is committed, and its sum sent, before the challenge that multiplies it.

\section{Zero knowledge}

Four things depend on the witness. Each gets its own randomness.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.4em]
  \item \textbf{Zerocheck rounds.} Round $i$ is the first to see $g_i$ and $g'_i$, which move its two scalars independently, so the round messages are exactly uniform (barring $\lambda = 0$ or an $r_i = 1$). This is Libra's mask~\cite{Libra} (a sum of univariates); the square term is needed because a round sends two scalars. A multilinear mask times a public factor $\prod_i p_i(x_i)$ fails: round $i$'s mask term vanishes at the root of $p_i$, where the message is the witness's own. The mask's wires sit outside the constraint system (in no row, $C$ zero on them), otherwise the witness part of the rounds would depend on them.
  \item \textbf{$\hat a, \hat b, \hat c$.} Each compression has six extra triples $(u, v, w = u \cdot v)$ with random $u, v \in \K$, read by nothing else. They make the three evaluations statistically uniform.
  \item \textbf{Lincheck rounds.} $\mu, \mu^2, \mu^3$ is a basis of $\E$ over $\K$ (unless $\mu \in \K$), so $t$ is a uniformly random table, and the whole lincheck is a function of $t$ and public values. Note that $\mle z(q)$ is never sent: only $\mle t(q)$ reaches the commitment.
  \item \textbf{The opening.} A WHIR opening shows Merkle leaves and folded tables. So $P$ is committed inside a table four times larger, the rest random, placed to match WHIR's layout (its first commitment interleaves 64 codewords, one symbol of each per leaf): $P$ fills the high half of each of its codewords' messages, so the random low half is a random lower-degree polynomial that makes the opened positions uniform; and half of the 64 codewords are random throughout, so the table left by WHIR's first fold (six variables) is uniform, and everything after that fold is a function of it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What is proved.} Honest-verifier zero knowledge with statistical error below $2^{-118}$, the hash being a random oracle only where it hides the unopened Merkle leaves. The simulator samples the sumcheck transcript directly (uniform scalars, a uniform $t$), then commits and opens any table that has the right value at the right point. The one hard step is showing that this opening is distributed like the real one. The full proof is in \code{crates/flock/membership-zk.md}.

\paragraph{What is not.} The Fiat-Shamir transfer (including WHIR's grinding nonces) is the standard programmable-oracle argument, not reproved. Nothing is claimed against a malicious verifier. The proof relies on this WHIR taking no out-of-domain sample on its first commitment; a commitment that includes one reveals a second evaluation, which would need its own mask. The prover's randomness must be uniform: the code takes it as input, and the benchmark uses a test generator. No concrete soundness bound is derived here.

\section{Cost}\label{sec:cost}

The verifier is an EVM contract (\code{crates/evm}). Its bytecode is generated from the Rust verifier, by running that verifier on a scalar type that records its operations, so the two compute the same thing; a test checks that they return the same claims on honest and on tampered proofs. It runs on revm with one added instruction, \code{FIELD\_MUL}, a product in $\E$ at 10 gas. It returns the two claims: the precompile that would check them does not exist, and each is counted at 20{,}000 gas. Both prices are assumptions.

A transaction with $b$ calldata bytes and execution gas $X$ pays $21{,}000 + \max(16\, b + X,\ f \cdot b)$, where the floor rate is $f = 40$ today (EIP-7623~\cite{EIP7623}) and $f = 64$ under EIP-7976~\cite{EIP7976}, proposed for the next fork. Measured on one proof:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & quantity & gas \\
\midrule
calldata, at 16 gas (4 for a zero byte) & 2{,}448 bytes & 39{,}084 \\
execution of the contract & & 73{,}472 \\
\quad of which \code{FIELD\_MUL} & 2{,}031 & 20{,}310 \\
evaluation claims, at 20{,}000 gas & 2 & 40{,}000 \\
\midrule
$16\, b + X$ & & 152{,}556 \\
floor, $64\, b$ & & 156{,}672 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The calldata is the 64 bytes of public input and the 2{,}384 of the proof. So the transaction costs \textbf{177{,}672 gas} under EIP-7976, where the floor binds (by a small margin), and 173{,}556 today, where it does not.

Two things the estimate by multiplication count misses. First, the multiplications are a minority of the execution: the rest is 173 hashes for the transcript and, mostly, the stack and memory traffic of feeding operands to a stack machine. Second, the contract is 32{,}339 bytes of straight-line code, above the 24{,}576-byte limit on deployed code; loops would shrink it and cost more gas.

The prover commits one table of $2^{26}$ words and runs two sumchecks over $2^{22}$ entries; the claim on the circuit is about public data, so anyone can open it.
